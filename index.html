The 4 percent rule pays the fee first

Two retirees run the same plan on paper: a million dollars, 7 percent a year, $40,000 of withdrawals, a fifty-year horizon, because retiring early makes fifty honest. One of them pays a 1 percent management fee. On a million dollars that fee is $10,000 in year one, a quarter the size of the paycheck it sits beside. It is the first expense the plan pays, ahead of housing and groceries, and it takes the same cut in every one of the fifty years, including the flat ones.

The drag works by subtraction on the return and multiplication on the outcome. Seven percent becomes six. Run both ledgers to the end: the fee-free portfolio finishes year fifty near $13.2 million. The one carrying the fee finishes near $6.8 million. Same deposits, same market, same $40,000 every year, and $6.4 million is missing. That gap is 6.4 times the starting balance, produced by a line item small enough to skim past on the statement.

The missing money comes in two parts. The fees themselves add up to about $2 million over the fifty years. The other $4.4 million is the compounding those dollars would have done, and the statement never shows it. Each year's fee prints as a modest number in a long column. None of the columns print the retiree's share of what the money was about to become.

This is also a withdrawal-strategy problem, not just a cost problem. The rule draws 4 percent and the fee draws another 1 from the same principal, so the true draw is 5. A plan drawn at 4 carries a margin against a bad first decade that a plan drawn at 5 does not, and sequence of returns is priced to the real draw, not the one in the budget spreadsheet. The fee moves the plan one full point closer to the edge without changing a single line of the retiree's spending.

The ledger: $1 million, 7 percent gross, 6 percent after the fee. $10,000 of fee against $40,000 of paycheck in year one, 25 percent. About $2 million of fees across fifty years, $4.4 million of lost growth on top, $6.4 million gone by year fifty. The statement prices the fee. Only the ending balance prices what the fee cost.
